Cohesion
• Network Density -is the number of ties in the

network expressed as a percentage of
ordered/unordered pair numbers.

• Average degree - Average number of ties per
actor.

• Average weighted degree centrality - is the
average of the weighted average degree of the
whole network.

• Average clustering coefficient: is a measure
showing the proportion of nodes that tend to
cluster together.

• Average path length is the average of the
minimum number of steps a node needs to take
to reach another node in the network. This
metric is used to calculate a network's efficiency
and shows the closeness of actors in a
collaborative network (Ortega, 2014).

The centrality
• The centrality  is a measure of how network structure 

and position contribute to the importance of a node 
(Opsahl et al., 2010).

• The most common metrics calculated for centrality are :

• DEGREE –  shows how well connected a particular node 
is, reflecting its direct influence as well as its popularity 
(Valente and Fujimoto, 2010). Most important are 
connections to actors that would otherwise remain 
isolated in the network. In general, most of them are 
connected to those in their immediate neighbourhood, 
to their group of friends (cliques). A→

• It can be divided into two categories: 
Indegree (which is the total number of sent 
connections) and Outdegree (which is the total 
number of received connections).

• CLOSENESS  measures the efficiency of access. A high 
value is interpreted as efficiency in communication, 
such as an actor's ability to disseminate and receive 
information quickly (Brandes et al., 2016).  

Nodes that discover the shortest paths to each other and 
are thus closest to any other node in the network are in a 
privileged position because they can monitor information 
flows and have an overview of what is really happening Source: Claudio Rocchini, 

inside the network. B http://www.rockini.name/math/images/centrality.svg

The centrality

• Connectivity (bridges) Those who act as brokers 
between two important groups within a network play a 
powerful role, but they are also points of vulnerability 
that can prevent or block the flow of information. 

• Betweenness calculates the extent to which a node 
lies between another pair of nodes. Such actors 
represent the people who connect two otherwise 
unconnected subpopulations, being actors with a 
high input of intermediation, brokerage, 
surveillance and information control (Abbasi et al., 
2012). C→

• Eigenvector shows the weighted graded measure in 
which the centrality of a node is proportional to the 
sum of the centralizations of the nodes to which it 
is adjacent →a node with a high value of this 
measure is connected to other highly connected 
nodes (representing a measure of power/influence) 
(Borgatti and Everett, 1997). D→

presentation of self in everyday life

• Self-presentation: When an individual appears in front of 
others, there is usually a reason for him to mobilise his

others, how he guides and controls the impression they form 
of him, and the kinds of things he may or may not do while 
sustaining his performance in front of them

the behaviours adopted to achieve goals (responses

Kelman (1959): 
social influence 
theory 

Individual knowledge and behaviour can be affected by
three social processes: conformity (normative influence of
others' expectations), internalisation (congruence of one's
own goals with those of other group members) and
identification (self-concept in terms of the defining
characteristics of the group).

They shape an individual's social identity;

Burgis (2021):Wanting: The Power of 
Mimetic Desire in Everyday Life

• insights from philosophy (Thomas Aquinas, Sartre, 
Rene Girard) and neuroscience; 

• Are we autonomous? Do we make only rational 
choices? Do our desires come from deep within? 
Are we above the influence?

• The models we encounter in our daily lives, be 
they celebrities, influencers, or even our peers, 
exert a profound influence on our desires. These 
are what we refer to as 'mimetic models '.

• Ecology of desire

• Mimetic desire typically works beneath the 
surface of our conscious awareness, influencing 
our decisions without fully recognizing its impact.

• Effects: It influences our feelings of self-worth and 
desires and catalyses cultural and political 
polarization.

• In the time of hyperimitation → social media has 
fueled mimesis because it encourages imitation by 
its very structure.
